A garage door cable rarely fails quietly. There is a bang, the door stops partway, and one side hangs lower than the other. If that just happened in your garage in {{city}}, here is what went wrong, what it means for the rest of the door, and what to do (and not do) until a technician arrives.

What the cables actually do

Every sectional garage door has two steel lift cables, one on each side. Each runs from a bracket at the bottom corner of the door up to a grooved drum on the torsion shaft above the opening. When the springs turn the shaft, the drums wind the cables and lift the door. The springs do the heavy work; the cables carry that force to the door. A double door weighs a few hundred pounds, so each cable is under real load every time the door moves.

Why cables fail

Most of the cables we replace in {{city}} fail for one of a few reasons:

  • Fraying and corrosion. Cables live close to the floor, where they pick up water, road salt, and whatever runs off the car. Over the years strands rust and break until the rest can't hold the load.
  • A broken spring. When a torsion spring snaps, the cables go slack in an instant, jump off the drums and tangle. People often call us about a "cable problem" that is really a spring problem.
  • Something under the door. If the door comes down onto a shovel handle or a bumper, one side stops while the opener keeps pulling the other. The cable on the blocked side unwinds and jumps the drum or wraps around the shaft.
  • Worn drums, rollers or bearings. A scored drum, a binding bottom roller, or a seized end bearing strains one cable until it gives.
  • Sloppy installation. A cable wound unevenly works loose over time.

What it looks like from the driveway

The classic sign is a crooked door: one side a few inches higher than the other, or the door jammed at an angle in the tracks. You may see a loose coil of cable near the bottom corner or a cable wrapped around the torsion shaft. Look up at the springs while you're there: a torsion spring with a visible gap in the coils has broken, and you have two problems, not one.

Why we replace both cables

Homeowners sometimes ask why we replace the cable on the side that didn't fail. The two cables were installed together, have run the same number of cycles, and have sat in the same damp corners. When one is worn through, the other is close behind, and a door with one new cable and one old one lifts unevenly. Replacing both keeps the door level, and the second cable adds very little to the job. Likewise, if a broken spring caused the failure, we replace the springs in pairs and set the cables at the same time, so you don't pay for two visits.

Please don't try to fix this yourself

This is the one repair we most want {{city}} homeowners to leave alone. The cables, drums and bottom brackets are all under the full tension of the torsion springs. Loosening a bottom bracket bolt, or trying to wind a cable back onto a drum while the spring is loaded, can release that energy without warning. Forcing a crooked door with the opener is just as bad: it usually bends a track or cracks a panel, turning a cable job into an off-track door repair.

Until we arrive:

  • Unplug the opener or switch it off so nobody presses the button.
  • Leave the door where it is, even partway open. Lock the door into the house and move anything valuable inside.
  • Keep children, pets and cars clear. A door hanging on one cable can drop.
  • Don't pull the red emergency release cord. On a door with a broken spring or cable, that cord removes the only thing holding the door up.

What the repair involves

Our technician first unloads the springs with winding bars so the door is safe to work on. The old cables come off, the door is leveled in the tracks, and new cables are wound onto the drums with equal tension on both sides. The springs are rewound, the door is balanced so it holds at waist height on its own, and the opener's limits are checked before we leave. Most jobs take about an hour.
