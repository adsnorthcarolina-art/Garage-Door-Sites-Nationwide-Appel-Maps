The first cold morning of the season is, reliably, our busiest day for emergency calls in {{city}}. Doors that worked fine in October stop halfway, openers strain and give up, and the bottom edge freezes to the slab. None of it is random. Cold does four specific things to a garage door, and most of them can be headed off in half an hour.

1. Steel springs lose strength as they get cold

A torsion spring is sized to balance the weight of the door at normal temperatures. Below freezing, the steel stiffens and delivers slightly less lift, so a spring that was already near the end of its cycle life suddenly can't hold the door. That's why springs snap on cold mornings far more often than warm ones. Check: pull the red release cord with the door closed and lift it by hand. A balanced door lifts easily and stays put at waist height. If it feels like dead weight or drifts down, the springs are weak and the cold will finish them. Spring replacement in {{city}} is a same-day job.

2. Grease thickens and rollers drag

Old lubricant on the hinges, rollers and torsion tube turns to paste in the cold. The opener then has to push through that drag on top of the door's weight. Check: wipe the tracks with a rag (tracks should be clean and dry, never greased) and put a light coat of silicone or lithium garage door spray on the roller stems, hinge pins and the spring coils. Skip WD-40; it washes grease off and leaves nothing behind.

3. The bottom seal freezes to the floor

Meltwater runs under the door, refreezes overnight and glues the rubber seal to the concrete. In the morning the opener pulls, the door doesn't budge, and either the opener's force limit trips or, worse, the bottom panel bends while the seal stays stuck. Check: if the door is frozen down, don't keep hitting the button. Release it from the opener and break the ice with a plastic scraper or warm (not boiling) water along the seal. A worn, cracked seal lets more water through; a new one costs little and fixes the drafts too.

4. The opener's safety settings trip

Openers have a force setting that stops and reverses the door if it meets more resistance than expected. Cold stiffness raises resistance, so a door that reverses on its own a few inches off the floor in winter is usually telling you the force limit is tight or the door is binding. Check: watch the door through a full cycle. Hesitation, jerking or reversing all point to balance or drag problems rather than a broken opener, and the fix is on the door, not in the motor. Turning the force up to compensate is a bad idea; it also disables the protection that keeps the door from crushing something.

5. Photo eyes fog and ice up

The two sensors near the floor on each side of the opening need a clear line of sight. Frost, condensation or a splash of road salt on the lens makes the opener think something is in the doorway, so the door will close a few inches and go back up while the opener light flashes. Check: wipe both lenses and make sure the small LEDs on both units are lit solid, not blinking.

Insulation and weatherstripping

An uninsulated steel door in {{city}} can leave the garage close to the outdoor temperature, which makes the rooms above and beside it cold and expensive to heat. Weatherstripping along the top and sides and a good bottom seal are inexpensive upgrades. If the door is due for replacement anyway, an insulated door with an R-value of 12 to 18 makes a noticeable difference and runs more quietly. See insulated door options for {{city}}.

If the door won't move at all

Look at the springs above the door. A torsion spring with a visible gap in the coils has broken; a stretched or dangling extension spring has snapped. Either way, don't try to lift the door, and don't let anyone pull a car out under it. Call {{phone}}.
